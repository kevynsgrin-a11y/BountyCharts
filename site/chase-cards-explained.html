What are chase cards?

Every set has a handful of cards the market hunts hardest. What makes them, and why they anchor everything else.

A chase card is the card a buyer is hoping to pull when they open a pack — the one with disproportionate value, demand, and pull-excitement. Usually it is the rarest printing of the most desirable card in the set: a premium treatment of a popular character or champion, a short-printed staple, or a card the competitive scene covets. Whatever the cause, the effect is the same: the chase card becomes the reason product gets opened.

Why chase cards anchor set value

Card value follows attention, and attention concentrates. In most sets, a small fraction of cards carries a large fraction of the total value — the chase cards and the tier just beneath them. This is why "expected value" calculations for a box lean so heavily on a few slots, and why the chase card's price effectively sets the price of sealed product: a box is worth roughly what the market will pay for a lottery ticket on its top pulls.

What makes a card chase-worthy

Three sources, often combined. Aesthetics: alternate art, special treatments, and low-numbered printings — collectible demand independent of play. Play: a format-defining competitive card, where the metagame drives demand. Character: popularity is its own economy; beloved characters carry a premium in any printing. The sturdiest chase cards combine all three.

Chase cards and price behavior

Chase cards move first and furthest in both directions. They lead set-wide price run-ups when a game heats up, they spike when results create new demand, and they take the hardest falls when a format shifts underneath them. They are also the cards where condition matters most — see the condition scale and the grading math before selling one.

Tracking a young game's chase cards over time — which is a large part of what this site charts — is the cleanest window into the game's economy. See the Riftbound price guide for how those forces play out in practice.
