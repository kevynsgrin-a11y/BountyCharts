When is card grading worth it?

Professional grading does not make a card valuable — it certifies value that condition already implies. The math for when that certification pays.

Grading services slab a card, assign it a condition score, and make that score tamper-evident. The service is real; the question is whether the premium a slab commands exceeds the fees, shipping, and wait. For most cards in most collections, it does not. For a minority, it clearly does.

The three major services

PSA is the most recognized name and generally commands the strongest premium for sports and TCG cards alike. Beckett (BGS) is known for a stricter sub-grade system and a following in certain TCG categories. CGC is the value option of the three, often with faster turnarounds and lower fees. All three grade the same underlying thing — centering, corners, edges, and surface — with different scales and reputations. Check each service's current fee schedule and turnaround before committing; both change.

The honest math

Grading costs: the per-card fee, shipping both ways, insurance on valuable submissions, and weeks-to-months of waiting. The payoff: the spread between a raw card's sold price and a graded copy's sold price at the grade you are likely to receive. That last clause is where people fool themselves. If an honest condition assessment says lightly played, do not model the near-mint slab price.

The rule of thumb: grading tends to make sense when the card is genuinely high-grade, genuinely valuable raw, and the grade-to-grade price spread is steep — the profile of a chase card in strong condition. It rarely makes sense for bulk, played cards, or low-value inventory.

Risks people skip

Three honest risks: you can grade lower than expected (the spread punishes this), fees can rise while you wait, and slabs are less liquid in a soft market than sellers assume. If you are grading to sell, grade the cards buyers already want slabbed — not the cards you wish were valuable.
